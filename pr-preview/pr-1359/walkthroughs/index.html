  1. Walkthroughs

Walkthroughs

Example applications that are production-ready, each with pages that build it step by step: the bar every one meets, which its pages close with as a checklist, and the three levels of tests it runs.

A walkthrough is an example application that is production-ready, with pages that build it step by step. Its code is a module of its own, at example/<type>/<name> in the repository, under the folder of its service type, and every sample on its pages is quoted from that code.

The walkthroughs

  • Shoppingcart: an API for shopping carts, behind JWT bearer tokens, that prices each item from a catalog service of its own over mTLS, both on PostgreSQL.
  • Wallet: a gRPC service for each caller’s own account, behind JWT bearer tokens, on a ledger service of its own that it calls over mTLS, which keeps the accounts and the transfers between them in PostgreSQL.
  • Billing job: a job, run daily by a CronJob, that bills a month of subscriptions in PostgreSQL through a payments API, once, however often it runs.
  • Order status processor: a Kafka processor that projects orders’ events into their statuses in PostgreSQL, produces each change of status, retries what fails for now through a retry topic, and dead-letters what never will.
  • Mailer: a RabbitMQ worker that sends the emails other services ask for through a provider’s API, retries what fails for now after a delay, and dead-letters what never will.
  • Databases controller: a Kubernetes controller that provisions a database and the role that owns it in PostgreSQL for each Database, writes the role’s credentials to a Secret, and drops both on delete, as the Database says.

The bar

Each walkthrough’s pages close with this checklist, and the application meets every item.

  • Settings: every one is sourced and checked, and a bad one exits 78, naming its variable.
  • Secrets: files mounted from Secrets and read through the Host’s FS, never environment variables, and none reaches a log or a span.
  • Certificates: files too, read again when they’re renewed, so a renewal needs no restart. A job reads them afresh on every run.
  • Availability: replicas and a PodDisruptionBudget, or, for a job, its concurrency and its retries.
  • Readiness: its probes. A job serves none.
  • Rollouts and shutdown: what SIGTERM does, the drain, and terminationGracePeriodSeconds against humus’s defaults.
  • Least privilege: a non-root container with a read-only root filesystem, and RBAC if it needs any.
  • Observability: traces, logs and metrics over OTLP, alerts on its instruments, humus’s own included, and a dashboard.
  • Failures say why, and failures are rehearsed, with failure injection.
  • Safe defaults.

Three levels of tests

  1. Units: the application’s own types and functions, as plain Go tests, inside synctest where they take time.
  2. The whole App, in process, with humustest.Start: on humustest’s Host where its dependencies can run there, and on humus.OSHost() against containers, behind the testcontainers tag, where they can’t.
  3. The deployed image, in a kind cluster, behind the functional tag.

The second and third levels run the same black-box cases, written once.
